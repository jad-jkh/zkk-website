Market sizing for startups

Overview

Sizing that shows its work

Market sizing for startups is most useful when someone else can follow it. An investor will usually ask how a number was built, and an answer of "a report said so" does not hold up well. Our Growth package, at $1,999, produces TAM, SAM, and SOM estimates with documented sources and assumptions.

The package builds on Launchpad, so the competitive landscape and market context come with it.

Founders who size a market themselves often use a top-down figure from a general report, such as a small percentage of a very large number. That approach is quick, but it is hard to defend, because the percentage is rarely tied to evidence. A bottom-up build is slower and easier to examine.

Method

How the estimates are built

TAM is the total demand for a product category. SAM is the part of it your product and go-to-market could serve. SOM is the share you could realistically obtain. Our approach is bottom-up where data allows: count the units or accounts, apply a price, then narrow by segment, geography, and channel.

A simple hypothetical shows the structure. If 1,000,000 potential buyers each pay $100 a year, the total is $100 million. If your product serves 20% of them, that is $20 million, and a 10% share of that is $2 million. In a real report each of those inputs has a source or a stated assumption.

For a longer explanation, read how to size a market for a new device and TAM, SAM, and SOM for medical devices.

Medical device markets add wrinkles, such as differing buyers and reimbursement. The method is the same, and the guides above walk through those wrinkles for devices specifically.

Buyers

Buyer and channel research

Numbers only mean something if you know who buys and how. The Growth package looks at customer segments, who makes the purchase decision, and which channels reach them. This feeds the SAM and SOM, because a channel you cannot access shrinks your serviceable market.

Adjacent markets are screened where they affect the picture, which helps when a first market is small but a neighbor is larger.

Where public data is thin, we say so and show the assumption used in its place. Readers can then change that assumption and see how the result moves. If you want a formal model that does this in a spreadsheet, the Comprehensive package includes a five-year revenue model in editable Excel.
